Pune's Ganeshotsav, mandal by mandal

Every Bhadrapada, Pune's old peths fill with drums, marigolds and ten days of public devotion. This guide walks you through the mandals that matter, in the order the city itself honours them.

Where it began

A festival built for the street, not just the shrine

Pune's public Ganeshotsav dates to 1893, when Lokmanya Tilak turned a household ritual into a citywide gathering — a lawful way for people to assemble under colonial rule, wrapped in music, oratory and devotion. That is why the festival still spills out of homes and into the peths: it was designed to be shared.

The city keeps a strict order of precedence among its oldest mandals, called the Manache Ganpati, or "honoured Ganpatis." Tilak himself settled the rivalry over who immerses first, giving that place to Kasba Ganpati as Pune's own gramdevata.

In order of honour

The five Manache Ganpati

These mandals hold a fixed rank in the immersion procession, a sequence that has stood for over a century. Visiting them in this order also happens to make a sensible walking route through the old city.

1

Kasba Ganpati

Pune's own gramdevata (patron deity) and the mandal granted first place in every immersion procession since 1894. The idol was found and enshrined by a local devotee, and the temple has anchored the festival's precedence ever since.

Kasba Peth · open in Maps ↗
2

Tambdi Jogeshwari

Named for the adjoining temple of Jogeshwari, regarded as one of the city's guardian goddesses. A fresh idol is made and immersed each year, and the pandal draws a strong turnout of women devotees.

Appa Balwant Chowk, Budhwar Peth · open in Maps ↗
3

Guruji Talim

Among the oldest mandals in the city, remembered for being founded jointly by Hindu and Muslim families — a story of communal unity that Puneris still point to with pride.

Ganpati Chowk, Laxmi Road · open in Maps ↗
4

Tulshibaug Ganpati

Set right beside Pune's oldest and busiest market, this pandal turns a shopping errand into a darshan stop — the two crowds blend into one on festival days.

Tulshibaug, near Laxmi Road · open in Maps ↗
5

Kesariwada

Housed at the wada that once served as Lokmanya Tilak's residence and the office of his newspaper, Kesari — a mandal with the festival's founding history built into its address.

Narayan Peth · open in Maps ↗
Beyond the honoured five

The mandals every visitor asks about

These aren't part of the formal precedence, but they're where most first-time visitors end up — for scale, wealth, or sheer spectacle.

Dagdusheth Halwai Ganpati

Pune's most visited and richest Ganpati trust, founded in 1893 by the sweet-maker Dagdusheth Halwai after he lost his son to plague. The mandal rebuilds its shrine into an elaborate themed structure every year, near Shaniwar Wada.

Open in Maps ↗

Mandai Ganpati

Set at the historic Mahatma Phule Mandai market, this pandal ties the festival to the city's everyday commerce — vegetable stalls and devotional crowds sharing the same lane.

Open in Maps ↗

Navsacha Ganpati

Known as the wish-fulfilling Ganpati, drawing devotees who come specifically to make or fulfil a navas (vow) — one of the old city's quieter but deeply devotional pandals.

Open in Maps ↗

Shrimant Bhausaheb Rangari Ganpati

Installed in 1892 by Bhausaheb Laxman Javale, this is widely credited as the first public Ganpati in Pune — a year before Tilak's citywide Ganeshotsav began. Each year's idol depicts a different mythological form.

Getting around

Tours & travels

The old peths are best covered on foot — lanes narrow further once the crowds and the loudspeakers arrive, and most auto-rickshaws won't get near the busiest chowks during peak hours anyway. Use an auto or cab only to reach the starting point of your walk.

How to reach Pune

By air

Pune Airport (Lohegaon, PNQ) handles domestic flights from most major Indian cities and a handful of international routes, and sits about 10–13 km from the old city — a short cab ride to Kasba Peth.

By train

Pune Junction is well linked to Mumbai, Delhi, Bengaluru, Hyderabad and beyond. The Deccan Queen and Deccan Express from Mumbai CSMT are the classic short-hop options; book festival-week travel early.

By bus

MSRTC state buses and private operators run frequently from Mumbai, Nashik, Kolhapur and other Maharashtra cities. The Shivneri AC service from Mumbai via the expressway is a popular, comfortable option.

By car

From Mumbai, the Mumbai–Pune Expressway covers the roughly 150 km in about 3 hours. Nashik, Kolhapur and Aurangabad connect via NH60 and NH48. Expect slower going and heavier traffic during the festival.

By bike

The Mumbai–Pune route through the Western Ghats — via the expressway or the older highway through Khandala and Lonavala — is a well-worn weekend ride for bikers heading in for the festival.

On the day

  • Wear comfortable, closed footwear — you'll be walking on packed streets for hours.
  • Go early morning or late night for shorter queues; midday sees the heaviest crowds.
  • Carry water, a small bag and keep valuables secure in the crush near popular pandals.
  • Stay in Deccan, Shivajinagar or the central peths to cut down on repeated travel.
  • Check each mandal's page or local news for closures and police diversions before you set out.
  • Treat every pandal as an active place of worship first, and a photo stop second.

Nearby and worth combining with your walk: Shaniwar Wada, the fortified Peshwa palace just a few minutes from Dagdusheth, and the lanes of Budhwar and Narayan Peth, where much of old Pune's daily life still plays out around the festival.

Eating your way through the peths

Famous food shops nearby

The festival has its own food rhythm — sweet prasad at the pandals, and old-city institutions to refuel at in between. None of these need a booking; you queue like everyone else.

Shrikrishna Bhuvan

Serving misal through the day in Tulshibaug since 1941 — the old-city standby, a short walk from the Tulshibaug and Guruji Talim pandals.
